
\section{Survey of epidemic modeling software}\label{sec:S1}

Here we provide the full search protocol and its results (\S\ref{sec:S1-search}), as well as the criteria behind
Table~1 (\S\ref{sec:S1-criteria}).
We also include summaries of each key package we identified (\S\ref{sec:S1-summaries}), whether or not it was included in the main table.
Finally, we discuss search limitations (\S\ref{sec:S1-limitations}). 

\subsection{Search protocol and results}\label{sec:S1-search}

We describe this as a structured, reproducible search rather than a PRISMA systematic review.
The goal is a repeatable procedure, rather than a synthetic assessment of the existing literature and software landscape. 

\textbf{Sources.} 
As mentioned in the main text, we searched software repositories (the CRAN Task View on Epidemiology, rOpenSci, Bioconductor, PyPI and the Journal of Open Source Software), Google Scholar, and conducted a GitHub topic search (\texttt{epidemics}, \texttt{particle-filter},
\texttt{data-assimilation}).
We also looked at the references of three key manuscripts on the topic (pomp~\citep{king_statistical_2016},
spatPomp~\citep{asfaw_tutorial_2024}, FlepiMoP~\citep{lemaitre_flepimop_2024}). 

We then conducted two broad literature searches using standard databases, PubMed and Lens.org.
For each, the full set of results was exported and screened with the same procedure. 
We chose these two databases for breadth and reproducibility.
Lens.org indexes preprint servers, the Journal of Open Source Software and conference proceedings in addition to standard journal articles, and both databases permit a full, scriptable export (Table~\ref{tab:S1-sources}).

\textbf{Search window and string.} 
We searched records 2020 onward, to focus on recently released packages. 
However, packages found through the search of software repositories were assessed regardless of publication year, which allowed us to retain well-tested comparators such as pomp (2016) and SimInf (2019). 
We constructed a three-part search string as blocks of terms (i.e., blocks \texttt{A AND B AND C}), with terms within each block joined by \texttt{OR}. 
On Lens.org the full search string was applied to title, abstract, keyword and field of study, and on PubMed it was applied to all fields.

\begin{itemize}\raggedright
\item
  \textbf{A (epidemiological domain):}
  \texttt{epidemic OR epidemiological OR "infectious disease" OR "disease transmission" OR compartmental OR metapopulation OR "meta-population" OR "state-space"}
\item
  \textbf{B (inference method):}
  \texttt{"particle filter" OR "sequential Monte Carlo" OR SMC OR "ensemble Kalman" OR "Kalman filter" OR "data assimilation" OR "partially observed Markov" OR Bayesian OR "Markov chain Monte Carlo" OR MCMC OR "approximate Bayesian computation" OR ABC OR "uncertainty quantification" OR calibration OR inference}
\item
  \textbf{C (signal of software):}
  \texttt{software OR package OR library OR toolkit OR "R package" OR "Python package"}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Inclusion and exclusion criteria.} 
We retained a package in the search if it 
(1) is a released, installable package or library; 
(2) simulates or fits
mechanistic compartmental or state-space epidemic models; 
(3) provides at least one built-in
method for Bayesian inference or uncertainty quantification (e.g., particle filter, ensemble
Kalman filter, particle MCMC, SMC\textsuperscript{2}, MCMC/HMC, ABC or nested sampling); 
(4) is available as of the search date; and 
(5) has documentation in the English language. 

We excluded
(a) agent-based or simulation-only tools with no built-in (public-facing) inference (e.g., Covasim, MetaWards, GLEAM, Starsim); 
(b) estimators of the pathogen reproduction number only (e.g., EpiEstim, EpiNow2); 
(c) general-purpose statistical or probabilistic-programming toolkits without specific capabilities built for epidemiological studies (e.g., Stan, PyMC, Pyro); and 
(d) packages with no release or repository activity in the last five years. 
Although we do not compare all packages that we found to \texttt{epymorph} explicitly in the main table, we provide more context below. 
The decision to not compare fully was based on epidemiological focus, regardless of inference method. 
An epidemic-specific package was counted whether it calibrated by MCMC, ABC or a filter, whereas a general Bayesian toolkit with no epidemiological model framework did not count.

\textbf{Screening.} 
We conducted screening of the full list of results in a three step protocol. 
(1) Each record's title, abstract and keywords were checked for the co-occurrence of three term sets: 
a \emph{software} set (package, software, toolkit, toolbox, library, framework, open-source, freely available, available at/from, GitHub/CRAN/PyPI, implemented in, R package, Python package, Julia, ``we present/introduce/develop''), an 
\emph{inference} set (particle filter, sequential Monte Carlo, SMC, ensemble Kalman, Kalman filter, data assimilation, Bayesian, Markov chain Monte Carlo, MCMC/pMCMC, approximate Bayesian, ABC-SMC, state-space, nested sampling, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, uncertainty quantification, quantified uncertainty, emulation, history matching, iterated filtering, parameter estimation, calibration, likelihood), and an
\emph{epidemiology} set (epidemic, compartmental, SEIR, SIR model, metapopulation, transmission, infectious disease, outbreak, forecast, and the major named pathogens: influenza, COVID, dengue, malaria, measles, mpox, Ebola, pandemic). 
Records matching all three sets were then reviewed manually.
(2) \emph{Double-check of records that were set aside.} 
We reviewed every set-aside record whose title named a package, and a random sample of the remaining set-aside records, to check for misses. 
(3) \emph{Manual screening and verification.} 
Each retained package's capabilities were verified by reading its documentation (package website, CRAN or PyPI page, or software paper), with a second author spot-checking the results.
We provide documentation of the decision-making process with the manuscript supplementary files.

\textbf{Database results.} 
The PubMed search returned \textbf{2,403 records}, of which \textbf{2,364} included abstracts and were screened. 
The keyword pre-filter advanced \textbf{437 records} for manual review. 
Most of these records were obviously not relevant upon inspection, such as clinical prediction models, network meta-analyses, phylogenetic and genomic pipelines, or disease-mapping. 
The PubMed screen yielded one new package meeting the criteria, summer2/estival~\citep{trauer_data_2025}. 

The Lens.org export returned \textbf{1,009 records} (663 journal articles, 298 preprints and 48 conference papers). 
340 were duplicates with PubMed.
Of the remaining, the keyword filter led to \textbf{208} potential sources. 
The manual review discovered one new package meeting the criteria,
MetaRVM~\citep{fadikar_developing_2025}.

\textbf{Recall audit.} 
The 1,927 PubMed and 461 Lens.org records that the keyword filter set aside were checked in two ways. 
First, we reviewed every set-aside record whose title named a package together with an epidemiological term (\textbf{101} records); all were out of scope or already known to us. 
Second, we reviewed a seeded random sample of \textbf{100} of the remaining set-aside records (seed $=20260724$), and found no additional packages.

\textbf{Outcome.} 
Beyond the two packages noted (summer2/estival from PubMed and MetaRVM
from the Lens.org preprints), the database search and the search on software repositories led to the same outcomes. 
Overall, the survey discovered 13 notable packages for comparison with epymorph, including the 11 in Table~1, as well as LibBi/rbi and epidemia (\S\ref{sec:S1-summaries}; Figure~\ref{fig:S1-flow}). 
We also summarize 22 related tools that fall outside the focus of the comparison (Table~\ref{tab:S1-context}).

\subsection{Criteria behind Table 1}\label{sec:S1-criteria}

In Table~1, a filled circle (\mkfull{}) means the package provides a dedicated API, construct or data service for the capability, and an empty circle (\mknone{}) means it does not. 
We recognize that some of these capabilities may be feasible if the user custom-codes something, but we do not count this. 
The feature must be built-in and natively supported with the current release. 

\textbf{Criteria.} 
The data rows require automated retrieval from an external source, or data that is provided within the software environment.
We also did not count a data set that exists only to run the package's examples, but that could not easily be re-purposed.
\emph{Model specification language} requires a domain-specific language or a custom-built declarative form for the compartments and transitions. 
\emph{Ready-made compartmental models} requires model objects
the user can use `out of the box'. 
\emph{Operator for demographic stratification} requires a construct that stratifies a model, not just an object the user fills in (e.g., a properly formatted matrix). 
\emph{Movement-based coupling} requires that individuals or infection
pressure are transported between spatial nodes, and this movement has some sort of method to declare the flow rate or patterns.
A mere correlation between nodes is not sufficient. 
\emph{Forecast generation} requires a function or method for producing forecasts from a fitted model, not something that would require heavy custom-coding from the user.

\textbf{Borderline calls.} 
\emph{Movement-based coupling.} surveillance/hhh4 is the closest
call: it has inter-unit transmission terms and a dedicated coupling API (\texttt{neighbourhood}
weight matrices, \texttt{W\_powerlaw} with an estimated decay parameter), but the coupling is a
fitted regression weight and nothing is transported, so it scores \mknone{}. epidemia pools
\emph{parameters} across regions through multilevel priors, with no cross-region transmission
term, so its regions are statistically linked but epidemiologically independent. pomp is
single-population; spatPomp is its spatial extension. \emph{Demographic stratification.} Five
packages do real work through machinery that is not a stratification operator, and all five
score \mknone{}: odin and LibBi supply array dimensions with indexed equations (odin's
documentation works through age- and vaccination-structured examples, making it the strongest
of the five), SimInf ships age-stratified template models but cannot stratify anything else,
spatPomp separates the spatial index from the within-unit state vector so that strata could
occupy the latter, and epidemia's group structure can carry strata. \emph{Inference.} LibBi's
extended Kalman filter is a deterministic Kalman variant, not an ensemble Kalman filter, so its
EnKF cell is \mknone{}. PyRoss's \texttt{evidence\_smc} is a density-tempered SMC sampler over
parameter space for computing model evidence, not a sequential filter over latent states, and
its latent-state inference uses a Gaussian (system-size expansion) approximation; its
particle-filter cell is therefore \mknone{}. SimInf's three calibration methods
(\texttt{abc}, \texttt{pfilter}, \texttt{pmcmc}) postdate its published description and were
verified against the current package. \emph{Forecast evaluation.} Only surveillance/hhh4
(\texttt{scores}, \texttt{pit}, \texttt{calibrationTest}) and pypfilt/epifx (continuous ranked
probability scores) score forecasts in-package. epymorph and FlepiMoP both leave scoring to the
forecast hubs and their dedicated scoring packages, which we judge the right division of labor
rather than a gap, so forecast scoring is not a row of Table~1.

\subsection{Package summaries}\label{sec:S1-summaries}

Packages compared in Table~1 are grouped as in the table. Packages that were assessed but not
tabulated follow, and Table~\ref{tab:S1-context} lists the remaining tools.

\textbf{Pipelines.}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{FlepiMoP} (Python and R): a meta-population compartmental pipeline configured in
  YAML, whose \texttt{compartments} and \texttt{seir: transitions} sections are a declarative
  model specification, with Bayesian inference by a custom MCMC with an EMCEE ensemble sampler
  and an integrated forecasting workflow. Its data layer ships US county populations, US
  commuting flows and global age-structure tables, and retrieves COVID-19 and influenza ground
  truth from the Delphi Epidata API and CDC FluSight. No particle or ensemble Kalman filter.
  The closest comparator to epymorph~\citep{lemaitre_flepimop_2024}.
\item
  \textbf{MetaRVM} (R): a meta-population compartmental package for subpopulations defined by
  geography, demographics or other strata, developed with the Chicago Department of Public
  Health. It calibrates by Bayesian optimization (a Gaussian-process surrogate with
  Thompson-sampling acquisition), applied in sequential phases as new weeks of data arrive.
  No particle filter, ensemble Kalman filter or MCMC, and no data
  acquisition~\citep{fadikar_developing_2025}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Toolkits.}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{spatPomp} (R): the spatiotemporal extension of pomp for geographic meta-populations,
  with a guided intermediate-resampling filter, block particle filter, adapted bagged filter
  and ensemble Kalman filter, and parameter estimation by iterated filtering. Models are
  hand-coded, with no built-in epidemiological structures or data
  services~\citep{ionides_iterated_2024,asfaw_tutorial_2024}.
\item
  \textbf{pomp} (R): inference for partially observed Markov processes, with particle,
  ensemble Kalman and ensemble-adjustment Kalman filters, iterated filtering, particle MCMC,
  ABC and Bayesian SMC. Single-population; models are hand-coded as C snippets; no data
  acquisition~\citep{king_statistical_2016}.
\item
  \textbf{odin/dust/mcstate} (R): stochastic state-space models written in the odin
  domain-specific language, with particle filtering, particle MCMC, SMC\textsuperscript{2} and
  iterated filtering; no ensemble Kalman filter. Reorganized in 2024 as odin2/dust2/monty
  (dust2 runs the filters, monty succeeds mcstate)~\citep{fitzjohn_reproducible_2021}.
\item
  \textbf{pypfilt/epifx} (Python): a bootstrap particle filter for online estimation and
  forecasting (pypfilt) with an epidemic modeling layer (epifx) that provides mechanistic
  infection models and CRPS scoring; used operationally for Australian influenza and COVID-19
  forecasting. Single-population, with no ensemble Kalman filter or data
  acquisition~\citep{moss_pypfilt_2024}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Simulation tools.}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{SimInf} (R): stochastic meta-population simulation by the Gillespie algorithm, with
  template models (SIR, SISe, SEIR), and calibration by ABC-SMC, a bootstrap particle filter
  and particle MCMC; not oriented toward sequential forecasting~\citep{widgren_siminf_2019}.
\item
  \textbf{PyRoss/PyRossGeo} (Python): Bayesian inference (MAP, MCMC, nested sampling) for age-
  and contact-structured compartmental models, with spatial commuting through the PyRossGeo
  companion. No particle or ensemble Kalman filter (see \S\ref{sec:S1-criteria}). Ships
  household, workplace, school and community contact matrices for several countries, which
  epymorph does not~\citep{adhikari_inference_2020}.
\item
  \textbf{surveillance (hhh4)} (R): endemic--epidemic regression for areal count time series
  with neighborhood coupling and one-step-ahead or simulated forecasts, fit by penalized
  likelihood; one of two surveyed packages that score forecasts
  in-package~\citep{meyer_spatio-temporal_2017,paul_predictive_2011}.
\item
  \textbf{Epydemix} (Python): single-population compartmental models with approximate Bayesian
  calibration (rejection, budget-constrained rejection, ABC-SMC). A companion repository
  supplies population structures and age-stratified contact matrices for more than 400 regions
  worldwide, the most extensive data layer of any comparator after
  FlepiMoP's~\citep{gozzi_epydemix_2025}.
\item
  \textbf{summer2/estival} (Python): a framework for constructing compartmental models whose
  defining feature is arbitrary stratification (age, strain, risk or user-defined), including
  mixing matrices and flows between strata, compiled through JAX. The companion package estival
  calibrates summer2 models through several backends behind one interface: PyMC (MCMC/HMC and
  SMC) and nevergrad (gradient-free optimization). The closest comparator to epymorph on modular
  model construction with swappable inference, but with no particle or ensemble Kalman filter,
  no movement model and no data acquisition~\citep{trauer_data_2025}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Assessed but not tabulated.} These two packages meet the criteria and were assessed on
the same rows, but adding them would lengthen Table~1 without changing the comparison.

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{LibBi/rbi} (C++/CUDA with an R interface): a state-space modeling language with
  particle filtering, particle MCMC, SMC\textsuperscript{2} and an extended Kalman filter,
  parallelized for GPUs and clusters. A general state-space engine whose epidemiological use is
  through \texttt{rbi.helpers}; development has slowed~\citep{murray_bayesian_2015}.
\item
  \textbf{epidemia} (R): semi-mechanistic Bayesian modeling with renewal-equation dynamics, fit
  by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo through Stan, estimating reproduction numbers, latent infections
  and intervention effects. A renewal model rather than a compartmental one; supports regional
  partial pooling but not movement-based coupling~\citep{scott_epidemia_2021}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:S1-limitations}

This survey is a snapshot, and package capabilities change. Re-verification changed seven
marks between June and September 2026, most of them after we read each primary reference in
full rather than in abstract, and several changed in a comparator's favour: pomp gained the
ensemble Kalman filter, SimInf the particle filter and two further calibration methods, FlepiMoP
automated surveillance acquisition and declarative model specification, and surveillance/hhh4
forecast scoring, while PyRossGeo lost the particle filter. The PyRossGeo case shows a hazard
of feature comparisons built from vocabulary: a routine named for SMC\textsuperscript{2} turned
out not to filter latent states, so we verified each filtering mark against the function's
documented behaviour rather than its name.

Binary marks sharpen the comparison but flatten real differences in strength: odin's array
dimensions and pomp's hand-written C snippets both score \mknone{} for demographic
stratification, though the first does much more of the work. \S\ref{sec:S1-criteria} carries
those distinctions. Our criteria, centred on mechanistic state-space inference, deliberately
exclude agent-based and purely statistical forecasting tools that some readers may consider
adjacent. A keyword pre-filter can only find packages whose abstracts describe what they do:
summer2/estival was missed by an earlier vocabulary centred on filtering and recovered only
once the inference set included \emph{calibration} and \emph{parameter estimation}, which is
why we rely on the recall audit rather than the pre-filter. Finally, we did not search Scopus
or Web of Science; a package indexed in neither PubMed nor Lens.org could in principle be
missed, although the two databases and the discovery sources converged on the same packages.
