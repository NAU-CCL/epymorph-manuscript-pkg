\section{The landscape of epidemic modeling software with built-in sequential inference}\label{sec:S1}

\emph{Draft for discussion: J. Mihaljevic et al., epymorph manuscript. Feature marks
verified against primary documentation as of 24 July 2026. The \S\ref{sec:S1-1} protocol has been
executed against both bibliographic databases under a single pre-filter lexicon; all counts
in \S\ref{sec:S1-9} and \S\ref{sec:S1-10} are reproducible from the exports and scripts in
\texttt{software-lit-search/}.}

Meta-population epidemic modeling draws on two software traditions that have historically
developed apart: statistical \textbf{inference toolkits} that implement rich families of
sequential Monte Carlo and Kalman-type filters for partially observed Markov processes, and
\textbf{end-to-end modeling pipelines} that package data handling, model construction, and
forecasting around a single estimation strategy. This appendix surveys representative
packages across that spectrum, with attention to how each package supports Bayesian
inference and uncertainty quantification and, in particular, whether it provides built-in
\emph{sequential filtering} (particle or ensemble Kalman filters) for online estimation and
forecasting. It then situates epymorph among them. Because probabilistic calibration is now
near-universal in epidemic software, we credit it broadly but treat sequential filtering as
a distinct capability, since online filtering is what couples calibration to rolling
forecasts.

\subsection{Software identification (protocol)}\label{sec:S1-1}

\begin{quote}
We describe this as a \emph{structured, reproducible search} rather than a PRISMA systematic
review: the aim is an auditable, repeatable procedure proportionate to a software-landscape
survey, not a formal evidence synthesis. We previously conducted a full systematic review of
spatial epidemic modeling \emph{studies}, reported separately in ref.~\citep{oshinubi_systematic_2026}.
\end{quote}

\textbf{Sources.} We searched, on a single verification date: (i) curated software
registries: the CRAN Task View on Epidemiology, rOpenSci, Bioconductor, PyPI, and the
Journal of Open Source Software; and (ii) bibliographic databases: Google Scholar, PubMed,
and Lens.org, the last scoped to include preprints alongside journal and conference records.
A targeted GitHub topic search (topics \texttt{epidemics}, \texttt{particle-filter},
\texttt{data-assimilation}) is used only to catch packages outside PyPI/CRAN, such as Julia-
or C++-based tools. We supplement automated retrieval with backward/forward citation
chaining from a small set of anchor papers (pomp~\citep{king_statistical_2016}, spatPomp~\citep{asfaw_tutorial_2024}, FlepiMoP~\citep{lemaitre_flepimop_2024}).

We use PubMed and Lens.org rather than Scopus or Web of Science because the target of this
survey is \emph{released software}, which is announced in venues the subscription indexes
cover poorly. Lens.org indexes preprint servers (arXiv, bioRxiv, medRxiv), the Journal of
Open Source Software, and conference proceedings alongside journal articles, and both PubMed
and Lens.org permit a full, scriptable export of the result set, which is what makes the
screen in \S\ref{sec:S1-9} reproducible from the archived data rather than merely described. That
this matters is demonstrable within our own results: of the two packages the database legs
added to the comparison, one (MetaRVM) exists only as a preprint and the other (summer2/estival)
is documented in an applied pipeline paper rather than a software-announcement article.

\textbf{Search window.} The keyword search is restricted to records from 2020 onward, since
its purpose is to surface recently released packages; foundational packages identified
through the registries or citation-chaining are eligible regardless of first-publication
year, provided they remain actively maintained (see exclusion (d)). Publication date is
therefore not itself an exclusion criterion; this keeps established comparators such as pomp
(2016) and SimInf (2019) in scope while still catching new entrants.

\textbf{Search string.} The three concept blocks are combined as \texttt{A AND B AND C},
with terms within each block joined by \texttt{OR}. On Lens.org the expression was applied to
four fields, title, abstract, keyword and field of study; on PubMed to all fields with a
2020-onward date filter. Registry and GitHub queries adapt this vocabulary to each platform's
syntax, since not all support full Boolean queries.

\begin{itemize}\raggedright
\item
  \textbf{A (domain):}
  \texttt{epidemic OR epidemiological OR "infectious disease" OR "disease   transmission" OR compartmental OR metapopulation OR "meta-population" OR "state-space"}
\item
  \textbf{B (method):}
  \texttt{"particle filter" OR "sequential Monte Carlo" OR SMC OR "ensemble   Kalman" OR "Kalman filter" OR "data assimilation" OR "partially observed Markov" OR   Bayesian OR "Markov chain Monte Carlo" OR MCMC OR "approximate Bayesian computation" OR   ABC OR "uncertainty quantification" OR calibration OR inference}
\item
  \textbf{C (artifact):}
  \texttt{software OR package OR library OR toolkit OR "R package" OR "Python   package"}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Inclusion criteria.} A record is retained if the software (1) is a released,
installable package or library (not a one-off analysis script); (2) simulates and/or fits
mechanistic compartmental or state-space epidemic/population models; (3) provides at least
one built-in method for Bayesian inference or uncertainty quantification (e.g., particle
filter, EnKF/Kalman variant, particle MCMC, SMC\textsuperscript{2}, MCMC/HMC, ABC, or nested sampling), with
sequential filtering methods (particle/ensemble-Kalman) recorded specifically; (4) is
available as of the verification date; and (5) has English documentation.

\textbf{Exclusion criteria.} We exclude (a) agent-based or simulation-only tools with no
built-in sequential inference (e.g., Covasim, MetaWards, GLEAM); (b)
reproduction-number--only estimators without a general state-space forecasting engine (e.g.,
EpiEstim, EpiNow2), noted but not tabulated; (c) general-purpose probabilistic-programming
or statistical toolkits without built-in epidemiological or meta-population modeling
structure, including Stan (via rstan/cmdstanpy), PyMC, and Pyro, which can fit epidemic
models but are general-purpose engines, recorded as context (\S\ref{sec:S1-5}) rather than
tabulated as competitors; and (d) packages with no release or repository activity in the
preceding \textasciitilde5 years. Note that the row-vs-context boundary is
\emph{epidemiological modeling focus}, not the inference method: an epidemic-specific
package earns a row whether it calibrates by MCMC, ABC, or a filter, whereas a general
Bayesian toolkit does not.

\textbf{Screening and verification.} Screening proceeds in three stages. (1) \emph{Automated
pre-filter.} Because bibliographic exports can run to thousands of records, each record's
title, abstract, and keywords are scored for the co-occurrence of three term sets: a
\emph{software} set (package, software, toolkit, toolbox, library, framework, open-source,
freely available, available at/from, GitHub/CRAN/PyPI, implemented in, R package, Python
package, Julia, ``we present/introduce/develop''), an \emph{inference} set (particle filter,
sequential Monte Carlo, SMC, ensemble Kalman, Kalman filter, data assimilation, Bayesian,
Markov chain Monte Carlo, MCMC/pMCMC, approximate Bayesian, ABC-SMC, state-space, nested sampling, Hamiltonian Monte
Carlo, uncertainty quantification, quantified uncertainty, emulation, history matching,
iterated filtering, parameter estimation, calibration, likelihood), and an
\emph{epidemiology} set (epidemic, compartmental, SEIR, SIR model, metapopulation,
transmission, infectious disease, outbreak, forecast, and the major named pathogens:
influenza, COVID, dengue, malaria, measles, mpox, Ebola, pandemic). Records matching all
three sets advance to manual review; the remainder are set aside. \textbf{The same three
term sets are applied to every source}; they are defined once in
\texttt{src/\allowbreak{}lexicon.py} of the search pipeline, and its \texttt{--check} step verifies that
this paragraph lists every term the screen runs on, so the reported protocol and the executed
screen cannot drift apart. (2) \emph{Recall audit of
exclusions.} The set-aside records are audited three ways: any record carrying software +
epidemiology signal but no inference term (a ``near miss'') is re-examined; every set-aside
record whose \emph{title} carries a software-name cue together with an epidemiology signal
is reviewed exhaustively; and a seeded random sample is drawn from the remaining set-aside
records to bound the miss rate in the stratum with no title cue. (3) \emph{Manual screening
and mark verification.} Advanced and recovered records are screened on title/abstract
against the criteria; each retained package's feature marks (Table~1, and
for the two untabulated packages the capsule in \S\ref{sec:S1-4}) are then verified
against its primary documentation (package site, CRAN/PyPI page, or JOSS/JSS paper) and
cited to that source, with a second author spot-checking the mark grid.

The automated pre-filter is a \emph{recall-oriented triage}, not the final decision: it
narrows a large export to a reviewable set, and stage (2) exists precisely to measure and
correct its misses. Within stage (3), records are dispositioned by rule where the
disposition is unambiguous (the record names a package already in the register, or falls
squarely within a documented exclusion criterion) and individually where the record requires
judgement; both the rule and the individual reasons are recorded per record in the annotated
screening CSVs. All dispositions are also recorded package-by-package in the candidate
register (\texttt{software-candidate-register.xlsx}).

\textbf{Reporting.} For each bibliographic database we report the number of records
retrieved, screened, advanced by the pre-filter, set aside, and audited, together with the
verification date (\S\ref{sec:S1-9}). Registry and citation-chaining sources are reported by the
packages they surfaced rather than by record count (\S\ref{sec:S1-8}).

\subsection{A modeling--inference spectrum}\label{sec:S1-2}

The most capable comparators sit at opposite ends of a spectrum. At one end,
\textbf{inference toolkits} offer broad algorithm suites but little modeling or data
infrastructure: the user hand-codes each model, and no epidemiological structures or
spatial-data services are provided. The R package \textbf{pomp} and its spatiotemporal
extension \textbf{spatPomp} are the archetypes, joined by the \textbf{odin/dust/mcstate}
stack and the \textbf{LibBi} engine. At the other end, \textbf{end-to-end pipelines} provide
modeling and forecasting infrastructure but commit to a single inference family:
\textbf{FlepiMoP} couples meta-population compartmental models to a Bayesian
MCMC/ensemble-sampler workflow, \textbf{MetaRVM} pairs a stratified meta-population model
with Bayesian-optimization calibration, and \textbf{pypfilt/epifx} delivers particle-filter
forecasting for single populations. Between the two poles sits \textbf{summer2/estival},
which couples an expressive stratified model-construction API to several calibration
backends behind one interface, but without sequential filtering, movement-based coupling, or
data services. A set of narrower tools targets specific model classes (\textbf{SimInf},
\textbf{surveillance/hhh4}, \textbf{EpiModel}, \textbf{PyRoss/PyRossGeo}, \textbf{Epydemix},
\textbf{SimBIID}). epymorph is designed to span the spectrum: it matches the inference
breadth of the toolkits, offering both a particle filter and an ensemble Kalman filter
behind one interface, while adding modular meta-population modeling and an automated
data layer that no toolkit provides. Data automation is not unique to epymorph, and the
comparison is sharper for saying so: FlepiMoP ships US populations and commuting flows
and pulls surveillance from public APIs, and Epydemix retrieves population structures and
contact matrices for more than 400 regions worldwide. What no other package retrieves is
the combination of surveillance, movement, and environmental data feeding one model.

\subsection{Feature comparison: criteria and evidence}\label{sec:S1-3}

\textbf{Marks.} The comparison itself is Table~1 of the main text, where \mkfull{}
means the package provides a dedicated API, construct, or data service for the capability
and \mknone{} means nothing in the package addresses it. This section records the criterion
behind each row, the borderline calls, and what was read to settle them. We do not repeat the
grid here.

\emph{There is no third symbol. An earlier version of this table used a partial mark, and it
proved uninformative: it covered both ``a language feature does the work'' and ``part of the
capability but not all of it,'' and it fell on 36 of the cells, including 10 of the 14 in one
row. Each row here therefore asks a single question sharply enough to answer yes or no, and
the borderline calls are argued below rather than hidden in a symbol. Two consequences are
worth stating. First, a mark is never a claim that a user could implement the capability:
every package surveyed is programmable, so that criterion would be met by all of them.
Second, where a package achieves something through general-purpose machinery rather than a
dedicated construct, it scores \mknone{} and the mechanism is described in the notes; odin's
array dimensions and SimInf's age-stratified templates are the two cases where that verdict
is closest.}

\textbf{Criteria.} The data rows require retrieval from a source, or data shipped for reuse
as model inputs; a dataset that exists only to run the package's own examples does not
count. \emph{Model specification language} requires a domain-specific language or a
declarative form for compartments and transitions. \emph{Ready-made compartmental models}
requires model objects the user instantiates, not worked examples to copy. \emph{Operator
for demographic stratification} requires a construct that stratifies a model, not an array
dimension the modeller can use that way. \emph{Movement-based coupling} requires that
individuals or infection pressure are transported between units by a modelled flow, not that
units are statistically correlated. \emph{Forecast generation} requires a facility for
producing forecasts from a fitted model; \emph{forecast evaluation} requires proper scoring
rules in the package.

\textbf{Inference methods offered (families).} epymorph: PF, EnKF. FlepiMoP: MCMC, EMCEE
ensemble. spatPomp: PF (GIRF/block/ABF), EnKF, iterated filtering. pomp: PF, EnKF/EAKF,
iterated filtering, particle MCMC, ABC, Bayesian SMC. odin/dust/mcstate: PF, particle MCMC,
SMC\textsuperscript{2}, iterated filtering. LibBi/rbi: PF, particle MCMC, SMC\textsuperscript{2}, extended Kalman. SimInf:
ABC-SMC, PF, particle MCMC. PyRossGeo: MAP, MCMC, nested sampling, and a density-tempered
SMC sampler for model evidence. surveillance (hhh4): penalized likelihood (frequentist UQ).
Epydemix: ABC (rejection, budget-constrained, ABC-SMC). summer2/estival: MCMC/HMC and SMC
via PyMC, gradient-free optimization via nevergrad. pypfilt/epifx: PF. epidemia: Hamiltonian
Monte Carlo (Stan). MetaRVM: Bayesian optimization.

\emph{\textbf{Notes on individual cells.} The ``probabilistic calibration'' row is
\mkfull{} across the board by design: it records that probabilistic calibration is now
near-universal, and it is the filtering rows, not this baseline, that separate packages.
surveillance/hhh4 quantifies uncertainty by penalized likelihood rather than Bayesian
methods but is credited here as providing probabilistic forecasts. On movement-based
coupling, surveillance/hhh4 is the closest call in the table: it has genuine inter-unit
transmission terms and a dedicated coupling API (\texttt{neighbourhood} weight matrices,
\texttt{W\_powerlaw} with an estimated decay parameter, time-varying weight arrays), but the
coupling is a fitted regression weight and nothing is transported, so it scores \mknone{}.
epidemia is \mknone{} for the same row on different grounds: its multi-region support is
partial pooling of \emph{parameters} through multilevel priors, with no cross-region
transmission term, so its regions are statistically linked but epidemiologically
independent. pomp is single-population, with spatPomp providing the spatial extension.
\textbf{On demographic stratification}, five packages do real work through machinery that is
not a stratification operator, and all five score \mknone{}: odin and LibBi supply DSL array
dimensions with indexed equations (odin's documentation works age-structured and age $\times$
vaccination models as examples, which makes it the strongest of the five), SimInf ships
age-stratified template models (\texttt{SISe3}, \texttt{SISe3\_sp}) but offers no way to
stratify anything else, spatPomp separates a spatial unit index from the within-unit state
vector so that strata could occupy the latter, and epidemia provides a group structure that
can carry strata. None provides an operator that stratifies an existing model.
\textbf{On inference}, LibBi's ``extended Kalman filter'' is a deterministic Kalman variant,
not an ensemble Kalman filter, so its EnKF cell is \mknone{}. PyRoss's sequential Monte
Carlo (\texttt{pyross.evidence.evidence\_\allowbreak{}smc}) is a density-tempered SMC sampler
over parameter space for computing model evidence, not a sequential filter over latent
states, and its latent-state inference uses a Gaussian (system-size expansion)
approximation; its particle-filtering cell is therefore \mknone{}, and the
``SMC\textsuperscript{2}'' of the PyRoss documentation should not be read as the state-space
SMC\textsuperscript{2} of Chopin et al. SimInf's three calibration families
(\texttt{abc}, \texttt{pfilter}, \texttt{pmcmc}) all postdate its published description and
were verified against the current package. \textbf{On forecast evaluation}, only
surveillance/hhh4 (\texttt{scores}, \texttt{pit}, \texttt{calibrationTest}) and
pypfilt/epifx (continuous ranked probability scores) score forecasts in-package. epymorph
does not, and neither does FlepiMoP: both leave scoring to the forecast hubs and the
dedicated packages those hubs use, which we judge the right division of labour rather than a
gap. Forecast scoring is therefore left out of Table~1 and recorded here instead.}

\subsection{Package capsules}\label{sec:S1-4}

\textbf{Inference toolkits.}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{pomp} (R): statistical inference for partially observed Markov processes. Ships a
  particle filter (\texttt{pfilter}), ensemble Kalman and ensemble-adjustment Kalman filters
  (\texttt{enkf}, \texttt{eakf}), iterated filtering (\texttt{mif2}), particle MCMC, ABC,
  and Bayesian SMC. Single-population; models are hand-coded as C snippets; no data
  automation~\citep{king_statistical_2016}.
\item
  \textbf{spatPomp} (R): spatiotemporal extension of pomp for geographic meta-populations.
  Filters include a guided intermediate-resampling filter, block particle filter, adapted
  bagged filter, and an ensemble Kalman filter (\texttt{enkf}); parameter estimation via
  \texttt{igirf}, \texttt{ibpf}, and \texttt{ienkf}, plus pomp's
  \texttt{pfilter}/\texttt{mif2}. Hand-coded models; no built-in epi structures or data
  services~\citep{ionides_iterated_2024,asfaw_tutorial_2024}.
\item
  \textbf{odin/dust/mcstate} (R; Imperial/MRC): stochastic state-space models via the odin
  DSL, with particle filtering, particle MCMC, SMC\textsuperscript{2}, and iterated filtering; no ensemble
  Kalman filter. Reorganized in 2024 as \textbf{odin2/dust2/monty} (dust2 runs the filters,
  monty succeeds mcstate)~\citep{fitzjohn_reproducible_2021}.
\item
  \textbf{LibBi/rbi} (C++/CUDA with an R interface): state-space modeling DSL with particle
  filtering, particle MCMC, SMC\textsuperscript{2}, and a (deterministic) extended Kalman filter, parallelized
  for GPUs and clusters; used for epidemics through rbi/rbi.helpers. No built-in epi
  structures or data automation; development has slowed~\citep{murray_bayesian_2015}.
\item
  \textbf{bssm} (R): general Bayesian inference for non-linear/non-Gaussian state-space
  models: Kalman and extended Kalman filters, bootstrap and extended-Kalman particle
  filters, and particle MCMC. Not epidemiology-specific; single time series~\citep{helske_bssm_2021}.
  \emph{(Context; not tabulated.)}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{End-to-end pipelines.}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{FlepiMoP} (Python+R; Johns Hopkins/UNC): flexible meta-population compartmental
  pipeline with no-code YAML configuration and an integrated forecasting workflow; Bayesian
  inference via a custom MCMC with an EMCEE ensemble sampler; no particle filter or ensemble
  Kalman filter. The \texttt{gempyor} engine takes user-supplied geography and
  mobility files, but the repository's \texttt{datasetup} layer ships US county
  populations, US commuting flows, and global age-structure tables, and pulls COVID-19
  ground truth from the Delphi Epidata API and influenza ground truth from CDC
  FluSight. Its YAML \texttt{compartments} and \texttt{seir: transitions} sections are a
  declarative model specification. The closest direct competitor~\citep{lemaitre_flepimop_2024}.
\item
  \textbf{pypfilt/epifx} (Python; U. Melbourne): a dedicated bootstrap particle filter for
  online estimation and forecasting (pypfilt), with an epidemic modeling layer (epifx)
  providing mechanistic infection models and CRPS scoring. Used operationally for Australian
  influenza and COVID-19 forecasting. Single-population: no spatial coupling, ensemble
  Kalman filter, or automated data acquisition~\citep{moss_pypfilt_2024}.
\item
  \textbf{MetaRVM} (R; developed with the Chicago Department of Public Health): an
  open-source metapopulation compartmental package for infectious-disease spread across
  subpopulations defined by geography, demographics, or other strata. It calibrates by
  Bayesian optimization (a Gaussian-process surrogate with Thompson-sampling acquisition),
  applied in sequential phases with checkpointing as new weeks of data arrive, and reports
  evolving parameter posteriors, so it does fit to data and quantify parameter uncertainty~\citep{fadikar_developing_2025}. Its profile is closest to FlepiMoP among the comparators (strong meta-population
  modeling with a real-time decision-support focus), but its calibration is a \emph{single,
  optimization-based} method: no particle filter, ensemble Kalman filter, or MCMC, and no
  automated data acquisition or symbolic/modular model specification. Surfaced by the
  preprint search (see \S\ref{sec:S1-9}); tabulated in Table~1.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Simulation- and calibration-focused epidemic tools (tabulated).}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{SimInf} (R): data-driven stochastic meta-population simulation via the Gillespie
  algorithm, with template models (SIR, SISe, SEIR). Calibration via ABC-SMC, a bootstrap
  particle filter (\texttt{pfilter}), and particle MCMC (\texttt{pmcmc}); not oriented
  toward sequential forecasting~\citep{widgren_siminf_2019}. All three postdate the
  published description, which covers simulation only.
\item
  \textbf{PyRoss/PyRossGeo} (Python): Bayesian inference (MAP, MCMC, nested sampling) for
  age- and contact-structured compartmental models, with spatial commuting via the PyRossGeo
  companion. Latent states are handled through a Gaussian (system-size expansion)
  approximation rather than a filter, so despite the \texttt{evidence\_smc} routine (a
  density-tempered SMC sampler over parameter space for model evidence), PyRoss provides
  neither a sequential particle filter nor an ensemble Kalman filter. It ships household,
  workplace, school, and community contact matrices for several countries, which epymorph
  does not~\citep{adhikari_inference_2020}.
\item
  \textbf{Epydemix} (Python): single-population compartmental models with approximate
  Bayesian calibration (rejection, budget-constrained rejection, ABC-SMC); meta-population
  structure is identified as future work. A companion online repository supplies
  population structures and age-stratified contact matrices for more than 400 regions
  worldwide, which is the most capable data layer of any comparator after FlepiMoP's~\citep{gozzi_epydemix_2025}.
\item
  \textbf{summer2 / estival} (Python; Monash EMU): a compartmental-model \emph{construction}
  framework with an epidemiologically expressive API for flows, force-of-infection
  multipliers, derived outputs, and, as its defining feature, arbitrary
  \textbf{stratification} (age, strain, risk, or user-defined), including mixing matrices
  and inter-strata flows, compiled through a JAX backend. Its companion package
  \texttt{estival} wraps summer2 models for calibration through several backends behind one
  interface: PyMC (MCMC/HMC and SMC) and nevergrad (gradient-free optimization). This is the
  closest comparator to epymorph on \emph{modular model construction with swappable
  inference}, and the only other Python package in the comparison to offer more than one
  calibration family. It differs on the axes that matter here: no particle filter or
  ensemble Kalman filter, hence no sequential/online assimilation; geographic structure is
  achievable by stratifying on location but there is no movement or commuting model; and
  there is no automated surveillance or demographic data acquisition. Documented end-to-end
  in an applied pipeline paper for Australia's 2022 Omicron waves~\citep{trauer_data_2025}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Bayesian regression / renewal-based tools.} These fit epidemic dynamics by
full-posterior sampling (typically Hamiltonian Monte Carlo via Stan) rather than sequential
filtering, and model transmission through regression or renewal equations rather than
mechanistic meta-population compartments.

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{surveillance / hhh4} (R): endemic--epidemic regression for areal count time series
  with neighborhood coupling and one-step-ahead/simulation forecasts; frequentist (penalized
  likelihood) rather than Bayesian, but the archetype of the regression class. It is also
  one of only two surveyed packages that score forecasts in-package (\texttt{scores},
  \texttt{pit}, \texttt{calibrationTest})~\citep{meyer_spatio-temporal_2017,paul_predictive_2011}.
  \emph{(Tabulated.)}
\item
  \textbf{epidemia} (R): semi-mechanistic Bayesian modeling via renewal/point-process
  dynamics, fit by Hamiltonian Monte Carlo through Stan; estimates reproduction numbers,
  latent infections, and intervention effects with full uncertainty quantification. Supports
  regional partial pooling but not movement-based meta-population coupling; no built-in
  filter or data automation~\citep{scott_epidemia_2021}. \emph{(Retained as the
  representative Bayesian epidemic package, and compared on the same criteria as the
  others, but not tabulated in Table~1, which is restricted to the twelve packages that
  carry the main-text argument.)}
\item
  \textbf{BayesianFitForecast} (R) and \textbf{Bernadette} (R) are recent Stan-based
  Bayesian tools in the same family: the former purpose-built for parameter estimation and
  forecasting of compartmental models, the latter for age-specific evidence synthesis of
  transmission dynamics~\citep{karami_bayesianfitforecast_2025,bouranis_bayesian_2026}. Both share epidemia's profile (Bayesian, single/regional,
  no filter) and are noted rather than tabulated.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Network- and individual-level models.} These represent transmission through explicit
contact networks or individual event histories rather than geographic meta-populations.

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{EpiModel} (R): network-based epidemic simulation using exponential-family random
  graph models; contact-network rather than geographic structure~\citep{jenness_epimodel_2018}.
\item
  \textbf{Pathogen.jl} (Julia): Bayesian inference of individual-level transmission-network
  models (SEIR/SIR) via a specialized MCMC that jointly infers the network, event times, and
  parameters~\citep{angevaare_pathogenjl_2022}. \emph{(Also confirms the value of retaining a Julia search branch.)}
\item
  \textbf{epinet} (R): Bayesian inference of epidemic and network parameters on contact
  networks~\citep{groendyke_epinet_2018}. Noted with the other network tools rather than tabulated.
\item
  \textbf{EpiILM / EpiILMCT} (R): spatial and network individual-level models of epidemics
  with Bayesian MCMC inference~\citep{v_individual-level_2020}. Surfaced by the PubMed screen; grouped with the
  network tools.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Simulation-focused and other tools.}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{SimBIID} (R): simulation-based inference for infectious-disease models: ABC-SMC
  and a bootstrap-particle-filter particle MCMC. No ensemble Kalman filter, meta-population
  structure, or data automation~\citep{mckinley_simbiid_2019}. \emph{(Noted; profile covered by pomp/SimInf.)}
\item
  \textbf{MEmilio} (C++/Python; German Aerospace Center/RKI): high-performance modular
  framework for multi-scale epidemic simulation (ODE, integro-differential, agent-based,
  hybrid, and meta-population graph models). The closest comparator to epymorph on
  \emph{modular meta-population modeling}, but calibration is limited to coarse-grained
  parameter estimation and machine-learning surrogates (no built-in particle filter,
  ensemble Kalman filter, or MCMC), so it is noted as a modeling comparator rather than
  tabulated on the inference axes~\citep{bicker_memilio_2026}.
\item
  \textbf{gemlib / gemlib.mcmc} (Python): an emerging probabilistic-programming library for
  epidemic state-transition (Markov) models: Gillespie and tau-leaping simulation plus
  Bayesian inference through composable Metropolis-within-Gibbs MCMC kernels~\citep{morariu_gemlib_2025,morariu_gemlibmcmc_2026}. A
  Python epidemic-modeling-and-inference toolkit close in spirit to pomp, but MCMC-only (no
  particle filter or ensemble Kalman filter), without data automation or a forecasting
  pipeline; a plausible future candidate for the comparison as it matures.
\item
  \textbf{tsgc} (R): forecasts epidemic trajectories with a dynamic Gompertz growth-curve
  model estimated by state-space methods and the Kalman filter, including wave/turning-point
  detection~\citep{ashby_forecasting_2024}. Phenomenological and single-series rather than mechanistic or
  meta-population.
\item
  \textbf{SPARSEMODr} (R): the authors' earlier spatially explicit stochastic COVID-19
  simulation tool; a predecessor to epymorph~\citep{mihaljevic_sparsemodr_2022}.
\item
  \textbf{covid-webapp} (web application; Numerus): a browser-based COVID-19 modeling tool
  built on a compartmental model with susceptible, contact, latent, asymptomatic, symptomatic
  and recovered classes, together with parallel response classes at reduced contact rates.
  Users supply incidence and mortality data, fit a chosen subset of parameters by maximum
  likelihood, and generate forecasts and counterfactual scenarios, with intervals from
  stochastic simulation or from user-supplied MCMC samples~\citep{getz_versatile_2021}. One of
  the few tools to put model specification, fitting and forecasting in front of a
  non-programming user, but single-population, with no movement-based coupling, no sequential
  filter, and no data acquisition.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{General-purpose filtering libraries}\label{sec:S1-5}

Outside epidemiology, several general libraries provide particle-filter and Kalman machinery
that can be applied to epidemic models with user-written glue code, but none supplies
epidemiological or meta-population structure: \texttt{particles} (Chopin \&
Papaspiliopoulos), \texttt{filterpy}, \texttt{PyMC}, \texttt{Pyro}, \texttt{Stone Soup},
and \texttt{SMCPy}. General probabilistic-programming toolkits such as Stan (via
\texttt{rstan}/\texttt{cmdstanpy}) are also widely used to fit ODE/compartmental epidemic
models, but by full-posterior MCMC/HMC rather than sequential filtering, and again without
built-in epidemiological or spatial structure. \texttt{ParticleDA.jl} (Julia) provides
distributed particle-filter data assimilation but is domain-agnostic. We note these for
completeness; they occupy the ``algorithm-only'' limit of the spectrum and are excluded from
Table~1. Agent-based frameworks with built-in calibration but no sequential filtering,
most prominently \textbf{Starsim} (Python; optimization- and surrogate-based calibration),
are likewise excluded as agent-based simulators (criterion (a)).

\subsection{Where epymorph sits}\label{sec:S1-6}

No surveyed package combines automated acquisition of surveillance, population, movement,
and environmental data, symbolic and modular meta-population model specification,
\textbf{both} a particle filter and an ensemble Kalman filter behind a single interface, and
an integrated forecasting pipeline. Filtering itself is not epymorph's distinguishing feature: particle
filtering is widely available (including, in Python, via pypfilt), and ensemble Kalman
filtering is provided by pomp and spatPomp. Nor is modularity alone, nor swappable inference
alone: summer2/estival offers expressive stratified model construction with several
calibration backends behind one interface, and spatPomp offers a particle filter and an
ensemble Kalman filter behind one interface over a geographic meta-population. What no
surveyed package does is put these together: the modular meta-population specification of
the pipelines, the sequential-filtering breadth of the toolkits, and an automated data layer
feeding both. epymorph's contribution is that \emph{conjunction}, within one Python
environment.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:S1-7}

This survey is a snapshot: package capabilities change, and marks were revised on
re-verification in two rounds, so all marks are stated as of the verification date. Between
June and July 2026 three changed: pomp gained the ensemble Kalman filter, SimInf gained the
particle filter, and PyRossGeo lost it. In September 2026, reading each primary reference in
full rather than in abstract changed four more, and all four went against our initial
reading of a comparator's favour or our own. FlepiMoP gained automated surveillance
acquisition and declarative model specification, both of which we had recorded as absent;
SimInf gained two further calibration families; and surveillance/hhh4 turned out to be one
of only two packages here that score forecasts, which epymorph does not do at all. The PyRossGeo revision illustrates a hazard of feature grids built from
vocabulary: PyRoss documents a routine named for SMC\textsuperscript{2}, but it computes model evidence by
density-tempered sampling over parameter space rather than filtering latent states, and the
shared name does not imply the shared capability. We therefore verified each filtering mark
against the function's documented behaviour rather than its name. Feature grids also
compress nuance, and our response to that has a cost of its own. We dropped the partial mark
because it had become uninformative, so every cell is now a binary judgement against a
stated criterion (\S\ref{sec:S1-3}). That sharpens the table but flattens real differences
in strength: odin's array dimensions and pomp's hand-written C snippets both score
\mknone{} for demographic stratification, though the first does substantially more of the
work than the second. The notes beneath the table carry those distinctions, and they are
part of the comparison rather than commentary on it. Our inclusion criteria, centered on mechanistic state-space inference, deliberately
exclude agent-based and purely statistical forecasting tools that some readers may consider
adjacent. A keyword pre-filter can only find packages whose abstracts describe what they do:
summer2/estival was missed by an earlier, filtering-centred vocabulary and recovered only
once the inference term set was widened to include \emph{calibration} and \emph{parameter
estimation}, which is why the recall audit rather than the pre-filter is the guarantee we
rely on. We also did not search Scopus or Web of Science, choosing PubMed and Lens.org for
their coverage of preprint, JOSS, and conference venues and for exportability (\S\ref{sec:S1-1}); a
subscription-index search could in principle surface a package indexed in neither, though
the two sources we did screen converged on the same tabulated set apart from one addition
each, and the registry and citation-chaining legs provide a third, independent route to the
same packages.

\subsection{Results: database screens}\label{sec:S1-9}

Both database exports were screened under a single pre-filter lexicon (\S\ref{sec:S1-1}), defined
once in \texttt{software-lit-search/\allowbreak{}src/\allowbreak{}lexicon.py} and applied unchanged to each source.

\textbf{PubMed.} The query returned \textbf{2,403 records}, of which \textbf{2,364 carried
abstracts} and were screened. The pre-filter advanced \textbf{437 records} in which the
software, inference, and epidemiology term sets co-occurred; \textbf{1,927} were set aside.
Of the 437, the great majority resolved as out of scope on title/abstract inspection,
overwhelmingly clinical prediction models (nomograms, risk calculators, prognostic scores),
network meta-analyses and cost-effectiveness studies, phylogenetics and phylodynamics
pipelines, genomic and proteomic tools, pharmacokinetic dosing software, and disease-mapping
or nowcasting utilities. A further group named a package already in the register, as
applications or citations rather than new software. The screen yielded \textbf{one
previously unseen package meeting the criteria for the comparison: summer2/estival}~\citep{trauer_data_2025}, a Python compartmental-model construction framework whose companion calibration
package exposes MCMC/HMC (via PyMC) and gradient-free optimization (via nevergrad) behind
one interface. It was surfaced by the inference terms \emph{calibration} and \emph{parameter
estimation}: the source paper describes its methods as ``calibration and optimisation
tools'' and never names a filter, so a filtering-centred vocabulary cannot see it. Several
single-population fitting, simulation and calibration tools were added as capsules or
context: \textbf{QuantDiffForecast} (MATLAB; parameter estimation and short-term forecasting
with quantified uncertainty for ODE models~\citep{chowell_parameter_2024}), \textbf{ARBO} (arbovirus modelling and
uncertainty quantification), \textbf{epidWaves} (multi-wave curve fitting),
\textbf{SimCOVID} (SIR/SEIR simulation programs), \textbf{hmer} (Bayes-linear history
matching with Gaussian-process emulation), \textbf{AlgebraicJulia} (modular model
composition, no built-in inference), and \textbf{EpiILM/EpiILMCT} (individual-level spatial
and network models), together with three review citations that inform the calibration and
reporting discussion~\citep{dankwa_calibration_2025,wieland_assessment_2026,chaturvedi_guidelines_2025}.

\textbf{Lens.org.} To reach venues PubMed under-indexes (arXiv, bioRxiv/medRxiv, statistics
and computer-science journals, JOSS, and conference proceedings), the same query was run on
Lens.org, scoped to title/abstract/keywords and 2020 onward, with journal articles,
conference papers, and preprints all included. The export returned \textbf{1,009 records}
(663 journal articles, 298 preprints, 48 conference papers; 97.5\% with abstracts).
De-duplication against the PubMed set removed \textbf{340} overlaps, leaving \textbf{669
novel records}, of which the pre-filter advanced \textbf{208}. Manual review confirmed the
register's known packages (EpiILM, Pathogen.jl, Bernadette, spatPomp, PyRoss, epidemia,
hmer, MEmilio, ParticleDA.jl all reappeared) and yielded \textbf{one new package meeting the
criteria for the comparison: MetaRVM}~\citep{fadikar_developing_2025}, an open-source metapopulation R package
with Bayesian-optimization calibration, together with three capsule-level tools
(\textbf{gemlib} and \textbf{gemlib.mcmc}, a Python epidemic probabilistic-programming
library; \textbf{tsgc}, a Kalman-filter growth-curve forecaster) and two context-level
packages (\textbf{eSIR}, a Bayesian hierarchical SIR forecasting toolbox in R~\citep{wang_epidemiological_2021};
\textbf{NGSSEML}, a general non-Gaussian state-space R package~\citep{santos_ngsseml_2021}). Rt-only and
general-purpose tools (EpiLPS, aghq) were recorded as context under criteria (b) and (c).

\textbf{Preprints were consequential.} MetaRVM appears only in the preprint stratum. Because
arXiv/bioRxiv/medRxiv are a primary announcement venue for computational tools, and several
tabulated packages (spatPomp, PyRoss, epidemia, LibBi, MEmilio) first appeared there,
including preprints was necessary rather than optional for a software survey in this field.

\textbf{Recall audit of the set-aside records.} The 1,927 PubMed and 461 Lens records set
aside (\textbf{2,388} in total) were audited three ways. First, the \textbf{near-miss
stratum is empty}: under the unified lexicon no set-aside record carries software and
epidemiology signal without an inference term, because the inference set already spans the
non-standard vocabulary (``uncertainty quantification'', ``parameter estimation'',
``emulation'', ``history matching'', ``calibration'') that a filtering-centred vocabulary
would miss. This is a structural property of the lexicon rather than a patch applied after
the fact. Second, an \textbf{exhaustive package-title sweep}: a released package almost
always names itself in its title, so every set-aside record whose title carried a
software-name cue together with an epidemiology signal was reviewed: \textbf{101 records},
all of which proved out of scope (phylogenetics and genomics pipelines, outbreak-sequencing
workflows, clinical prediction tools, mobile applications, reproduction-number utilities,
agent-based simulators) or already in the register (flepiMoP, SimCOVID, epidWaves, nosoi).
\textbf{Zero missed in-scope packages.} Third, an \textbf{unbiased random-sample audit}:
from the \textbf{1,585} set-aside records carrying no package-title cue, a seeded random
sample of \textbf{100} (seed 20260724, reproducible via \texttt{src/recall.py}) was reviewed
by title, again finding \textbf{no in-scope forecasting package}; the sample was dominated
by nomograms, prognostic models, network meta-analyses and pharmacokinetic studies. Zero
misses in 100 gives a rule-of-three upper 95\% bound of roughly 3\% on that stratum, and the
exhaustive title sweep makes the effective miss rate for our target, mechanistic
meta-population forecasting packages, lower still.

\textbf{Outcome.} Screening 3,033 unique records under one lexicon added \textbf{two
packages to the comparison} (summer2/estival from PubMed, MetaRVM from the Lens preprint
stratum) and a further set of single-population, general-purpose, network-level and
calibration tools recorded as capsules or context. Both additions are informative about the
boundary the comparison draws: each offers modular model construction and more than one
calibration route, and neither offers sequential filtering, movement-based spatial coupling,
or automated data acquisition. Full per-record dispositions are in
\texttt{software-lit-search/\allowbreak{}results/\allowbreak{}shortlist-pubmed.csv} and
\texttt{results/shortlist-lens.csv}, with the audit strata in
\texttt{results/\allowbreak{}recall-titlesweep.csv} and \texttt{results/recall-sample.csv}; every tool is
also in \texttt{software-candidate-register.xlsx}.

\emph{Note on the first pass.} An earlier execution of this review (July 2026) inadvertently
applied three different pre-filter lexicons across its three legs, and staged the Lens
retrieval in two runs because the first hit the free-account export cap. The screen reported
here supersedes it: one lexicon, one Lens export (verified to contain every record of the
two earlier runs and nothing further), applied to both sources. The superseded scripts and
outputs are retained in \texttt{software-lit-search/} for provenance. Re-screening under the
unified lexicon recovered one package (summer2/estival) that the narrower vocabulary had
missed; it did not overturn any disposition made in the first pass.

\subsection{Study selection flow}\label{sec:S1-10}

Identification proceeded through two streams. \emph{Bibliographic databases} contributed
2,403 records from PubMed and 1,009 from Lens.org; after removing 340 PubMed--Lens

manual review (437 PubMed + 208 Lens). The 2,388 records set aside were not discarded
blindly: a three-part recall audit (an empty near-miss stratum by construction, an
exhaustive package-title sweep of every set-aside record, and a seeded random-sample check)
confirmed \textbf{zero missed in-scope packages}. Advanced and recovered records, together
with the ``other source'' tools, were assessed against the inclusion criteria and had their
feature marks verified against primary documentation.

The review \textbf{retained 14 packages for the feature comparison}, twelve of them
tabulated in Table~1 and two (LibBi/rbi, epidemia) described in \S\ref{sec:S1-4} only,
\textbf{22 further
packages} recorded as capsules or context, and \textbf{3 method/review references}; the
