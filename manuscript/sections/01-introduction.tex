\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Meta-population models, in which spatially distinct sub-populations are connected by host
movement, are among the most widely used tools for understanding regional infectious disease
dynamics and for generating the forecasts on which public health decisions increasingly
depend~\citep{grenfell_metapopulation_1997,keeling_modeling_2011,riley_large-scale_2007,rohani_opposite_1999}. During the COVID-19 pandemic, these models proved instrumental in projecting
disease burden and evaluating intervention strategies across geographic scales from counties
to nations~\citep{pei_differential_2020,cramer_evaluation_2022,bertozzi_challenges_2020}. Yet no existing software environment supports the full lifecycle of a
meta-population modeling study, from model construction through parameter estimation to
operational forecasting, within a single, modular framework. In practice, most researchers
build bespoke implementations in which assumptions about disease transmission, host
movement, and geographic structure are intertwined throughout custom codebases, making it
difficult to compare alternative model formulations, adapt models to new pathogens, or
reproduce published analyses~\citep{willem_lessons_2017,pollett_recommended_2021}.

Spatial epidemic models suffer from pervasive issues with both reproducibility and
comparability. Our own systematic review of 109 spatial epidemiological studies of COVID-19
found that only 30.6\% provided accessible code~\citep{oshinubi_systematic_2026}, and another study could only
completely reproduce 4 of 100 randomly sampled COVID-19 modeling studies~\citep{henderson_reproducibility_2024}, a problem
now recognized widely enough to have prompted a 22-element framework for reproducibility in
computational epidemiology that current studies rarely satisfy~\citep{pokutnaya_implementation_2023}. These
reproducibility failures compound a deeper comparability problem. Meta-population models
exhibit high-dimensional state spaces and complex spatial interdependencies that necessitate
sophisticated inference algorithms such as particle filters or ensemble Kalman filters~\citep{king_statistical_2016,ganyani_simulation_2021,ionides_iterated_2024}, yet implementing these methods requires substantial custom code for each model
structure~\citep{asfaw_tutorial_2024,ning_iterated_2023,funk_choices_2020}. A result is that multi-model forecasting efforts, such as the CDC
FluSight challenge~\citep{cdc_flusight_2026,loo_us_2024} cannot systematically isolate which modeling choices drive
differences in forecast accuracy~\citep{shea_harnessing_2020,reich_collaborative_2022}. While ensembling models improve forecast quality~\citep{howerton_evaluation_2023,reich_collaborative_2019}, the inability to vary individual components (compartmental structure, movement
assumptions, spatial resolution) while holding others constant, limits the field's ability
to learn which structural elements of spatial, mechanistic models most affect forecasts.
What is needed is modular software infrastructure that integrates the full forecasting
workflow, from data acquisition through model specification, calibration, and probabilistic
prediction, as a customizable but highly reproducible pipeline~\citep{lutz_applying_2019,moran_future_2025,birrell_evidence_2018}.

Existing software addresses individual components of this workflow, but none spans it in
full (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}), and the most capable systems sit at opposite ends of a spectrum. At one
end, statistical inference toolkits, \texttt{pomp} and its spatiotemporal extension
\texttt{spatPomp}~\citep{king_statistical_2016,ionides_iterated_2024,asfaw_tutorial_2024}, the \texttt{odin2}/\texttt{dust2}/\texttt{monty} stack~\citep{fitzjohn_reproducible_2021},
and \texttt{LibBi} with its \texttt{rbi} interface~\citep{murray_bayesian_2015}, implement broad collections of
fitting algorithms for partially observed Markov processes. \texttt{spatPomp} offers both a
particle filter and an ensemble Kalman filter for geographic meta-populations~\citep{ionides_iterated_2024,ning_iterated_2023}.
However, what these toolkits lack is modeling and data infrastructure: none ships an
epidemiological model library, none retrieves data of any kind, and every model needs
to be hand-coded (e.g, C snippets in \texttt{pomp}
and \texttt{spatPomp}), which raises the barrier to entry. At the other end of the spectrum,
epidemiology-specific pipelines supply domain-specific infrastructure but commit to a single
inference strategy or a single model class. \texttt{FlepiMoP} couples meta-population
compartmental models to Bayesian forecasting through a single Markov chain Monte Carlo
scheme, and is the one comparator with a data layer of comparable ambition: its
repository ships US county populations and commuting flows and pulls COVID-19 and
influenza ground truth from public APIs, though only for the United States~\citep{lemaitre_flepimop_2024}. \texttt{Epydemix} offers single-population
compartmental models with approximate Bayesian calibration and no meta-population
structure, but retrieves population structures and contact matrices for more than 400
regions worldwide~\citep{gozzi_epydemix_2025}. And \texttt{summer2}, allows multiple calibration backends through its companion
package \texttt{estival} but provides no sequential filtering, no movement-based spatial
coupling, and no automated data acquisition~\citep{trauer_data_2025}. Moreover, appendix S1 (Table S1)
reports a reproducible, systematic comparison across the full software landscape.

Bluntly, no existing package combines modular meta-population model construction, automated
acquisition of demographic and surveillance data, multiple interchangeable inference
algorithms, and an integrated forecasting pipeline within a single environment. Here we
introduce \texttt{epymorph}, a Python package that provides this unified framework.
\texttt{epymorph} implements a Geography, Population, Movement (GPM) framework~\citep{oshinubi_systematic_2026},
under which a spatial model is decomposed into three interacting sub-models: an
intra-population model (IPM) describing transmission and disease dynamics, including
multi-strata structures such as age groups or multi-species transmission; a movement model
(MM) representing how hosts move between spatially distinct sub-populations, from gravity
kernels to empirical commuting flows; and a geographic model (GEO) supplying the attributes
of the geography in which the model is deployed (e.g., sub-population sizes, locations, and
local environmental and demographic features). \texttt{epymorph} assembles these sub-models
into a single self-contained object that allows for model execution and calibration. Because
the components are modular, a novel compartmental model can be deployed in a new geography,
or its distance-based movement dynamics swapped for commuter-style movement, all by changing
a single line of code.

For U.S.-based studies, \texttt{epymorph} automates the acquisition of Census population
data at resolutions from state to census block group, empirical commuting flows, PRISM
climate data, and CDC surveillance data, removing the requirement that users be experts in
munging diverse data sources. These data components supply covariate data to run a model and
observational data for model-fitting, with each quantity returned conformed to the study's
geographic scope and time frame. Studies outside the United States can use the same
modeling, inference, and forecasting machinery, by supplying custom data through the same
interface. Compiled models are executed as stochastic simulations and fit to observations
using a choice of particle filtering~\citep{gordon_novel_1993,arulampalam_tutorial_2002} or ensemble Kalman filtering~\citep{evensen_ensemble_2003,lal_application_2021,abbas_joint_2025},
accommodating stratified data and multiple simultaneous surveillance streams. Fitted models
then feed a probabilistic forecasting pipeline that can, for example, simulate the effects
of local or regional interventions.
