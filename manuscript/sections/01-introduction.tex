\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Meta-population models, in which spatially distinct sub-populations are connected by host movement, are among the most widely used tools for understanding regional infectious disease dynamics and for generating the forecasts on which public health decisions increasingly depend~\citep{grenfell_metapopulation_1997,keeling_modeling_2011,riley_large-scale_2007,rohani_opposite_1999}. 
During the COVID-19 pandemic, these models proved instrumental in projecting disease burden and evaluating intervention strategies across geographic scales from counties to nations~\citep{pei_differential_2020,cramer_evaluation_2022,bertozzi_challenges_2020}. 
Yet no existing software environment supports the full life cycle of a
meta-population modeling study, from model construction through parameter estimation to operational forecasting, within a modular framework that allows users choices of advanced approaches to modeling and statistical inference. 
In practice, most researchers build bespoke implementations of spatial models in which assumptions about disease transmission, host movement, and geographic structure are intertwined throughout custom code bases, making it difficult to compare alternative model formulations, adapt models to new pathogens, or even reproduce published analyses~\citep{willem_lessons_2017,pollett_recommended_2021}.

Spatial epidemic models suffer from issues with both reproducibility and
comparability. A pivotal study could only completely reproduce 4 of 100 randomly sampled COVID-19 modeling studies~\citep{henderson_reproducibility_2024}, a problem now recognized widely enough to have prompted a 22-element framework for reproducibility in
computational epidemiology that current studies rarely satisfy~\citep{pokutnaya_implementation_2023}. 
Our own systematic review of 109 spatial epidemiological studies of COVID-19 found that only 30.6\% provided accessible code~\citep{oshinubi_systematic_2026}.
These reproducibility failures compound a deeper comparability problem. Meta-population models exhibit high-dimensional state spaces and complex spatial interdependencies that necessitate sophisticated inference algorithms such as particle filters or ensemble Kalman filters~\citep{king_statistical_2016,ganyani_simulation_2021,ionides_iterated_2024}, yet implementing these methods requires substantial custom code for each model structure~\citep{asfaw_tutorial_2024,ning_iterated_2023,funk_choices_2020}. A result is that multi-model forecasting efforts, such as the CDC
FluSight challenge~\citep{cdc_flusight_2026,loo_us_2024} cannot systematically isolate which modeling choices drive differences in forecast accuracy~\citep{shea_harnessing_2020,reich_collaborative_2022}. 
While ensembling models improve forecast quality~\citep{howerton_evaluation_2023,reich_collaborative_2019}, the inability to vary individual components (compartmental structure, movement
assumptions, spatial resolution) while holding others constant, limits the field's ability to learn which structural elements of spatial, mechanistic models most affect forecasts.
What is needed is modular software infrastructure that integrates the full forecasting workflow, from data acquisition through model specification, calibration, and probabilistic prediction, as a customizable but highly reproducible pipeline~\citep{lutz_applying_2019,moran_future_2025,birrell_evidence_2018}.

Existing software addresses individual components of this workflow, but none spans it in full (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}). On one hand, statistical inference toolkits that are powerful but not tailored to epidemiology, such as \texttt{pomp} and its spatiotemporal extension \texttt{spatPomp}~\citep{king_statistical_2016,ionides_iterated_2024,asfaw_tutorial_2024}, the \texttt{odin2}/\texttt{dust2}/\texttt{monty} stack~\citep{fitzjohn_reproducible_2021}, and \texttt{LibBi} with its \texttt{rbi} interface~\citep{murray_bayesian_2015}, implement broad collections of fitting algorithms for partially observed Markov processes. For instance, \texttt{spatPomp} offers both a particle filter and an ensemble Kalman filter for geographic meta-populations~\citep{ionides_iterated_2024,ning_iterated_2023}.
However, what these toolkits lack is modeling and data infrastructure: none prove an epidemiological model library, none retrieves data of any kind, and every model needs to be hand-coded (e.g, C snippets), which raises the barrier to entry. 
On the other hand, epidemiology-specific pipelines supply domain-specific infrastructure but most often commit to a single inference strategy or a single model class. 
\texttt{FlepiMoP} is an outstanding example that couples meta-population compartmental models to Bayesian forecasting through a Markov chain Monte Carlo scheme and offers access to some data that is essential for epidemiological studies~\citep{lemaitre_flepimop_2024}. 
\texttt{Epydemix} provides compartmental disease models with approximate Bayesian calibration, and provides contact matrices for more than 400
regions worldwide, but there is no support for meta-population
or spatial models~\citep{gozzi_epydemix_2025}. 
And \texttt{summer2}, allows multiple calibration backends through its companion package \texttt{estival} but provides no sequential filtering, no movement-based spatial coupling, and no automated data acquisition~\citep{trauer_data_2025}. 
In our supplementary materials, we provide a structured, reproducible comparison across the full software landscape (Methods).

Bluntly, no existing package combines modular meta-population model construction, automated acquisition of demographic and surveillance data, multiple interchangeable inference algorithms, and an integrated forecasting pipeline within a single software environment (Table~\ref{tab:comparison}). 
Here we introduce \texttt{epymorph}, a Python package that provides this unified framework.
\texttt{epymorph} implements a Geography, Population, Movement (GPM) framework~\citep{oshinubi_systematic_2026}, under which a spatial model is decomposed into three interacting sub-models: an intra-population model (IPM) describing transmission and disease dynamics via a compartment model, including multi-strata structures such as age groups or multi-species transmission; a movement model (MM) representing how hosts or vectors move between spatially distinct sub-populations; and a geographic model (GEO) supplying the attributes of the geography in which the model is deployed (e.g., sub-population counts, local environmental and demographic features) (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}).
\texttt{epymorph} assembles these sub-models into a single self-contained object that allows for model execution and calibration. 
Because the components are modular, a novel compartmental model can be deployed in a new geography, or distance-based movement dynamics can ve swapped for commuter movement driven by real data, all by changing
a single line of code.

For U.S.-based studies, \texttt{epymorph} automates the acquisition of Census population data at resolutions from state to census block group, empirical commuting flows, PRISM climate data, and CDC surveillance data, removing the requirement that users be experts in munging diverse data sources. 
Our internal data machinery ensures that model inputs and outputs are harmonized, such that each quantity returned conforms to the study's geographic scope and time frame. 
Importantly, studies outside the United States, or studies wanting to use custom geographic scopes, can still leverage the same modeling, inference, and forecasting machinery, by supplying custom data through the same
interface. 
This effectively exposes epymorph's simulation and inference framework to any spatial modeling domain based around compartmental models. 
Compiled models are executed as stochastic simulations and fit to observations using a choice of particle filtering~\citep{gordon_novel_1993,arulampalam_tutorial_2002} or ensemble Kalman filtering~\citep{evensen_ensemble_2003,lal_application_2021,abbas_joint_2025}, accommodating stratified data and multiple simultaneous surveillance streams (e.g., fitting to cases and hospitalizations). 
Fitted models then feed a probabilistic forecasting pipeline that can, for example, simulate the effects
of locally-refined interventions through the forecast window. 

Here we demonstrate key features of \texttt{epymorph} with two case studies, each of which compares competing models by running them through the an advanced forecasting pipeline.
First, we test whether age-stratification and empirically derived contact mixing improve forecasts of influenza hospitalizations across the United States during the 2025-2026 season. 
Second, we test the roles of spatial resolution and host movement in forecasting SARS-CoV-2 hospitalizations and deaths in three New England states during the 2020-2021 winter wave. 
We find that empirical age-structured contact mixing improves influenza forecasts, increasingly so at longer horizons, whereas for SARS-CoV-2 a coarser State-level model outperforms county-level models at forecasting State-level data, although empirical commuting improves county-level forecasts relative to no movement.
By making model components interchangeable, automating spatial and surveillance data workflows, and exposing multiple inference algorithms through a common interface, \texttt{epymorph} converts comparisons that previously required rewriting bespoke codebases into controlled computational experiments. 
\texttt{epymorph} provide the standardized, reproducible, and rapidly deployable modeling infrastructure that collaborative forecasting and pandemic preparedness demand~\citep{howerton_evaluation_2023,shea_harnessing_2020,reich_collaborative_2022}.
